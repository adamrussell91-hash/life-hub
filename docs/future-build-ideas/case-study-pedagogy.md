# Case Study as a Pedagogy Mode

A design for using the case study as a lesson type: its purpose, the research behind it, and a modular flow made of swappable stages.

**Status:** concept doc. Nothing builds from this yet. Sits beside "Teaching Categories: A Nested Hierarchy" in Notion, where Case Study is listed as an Extended (multi-session) pedagogical mode: *analysis of real-world scenarios to develop reasoning and decision-making skills*.

---

## 1. Purpose (backwards mapping)

A case study exists to **build judgment in situations that don't resolve cleanly**. Students get a bounded, real situation and reason from its particulars to a decision or explanation, then carry that reasoning to the next situation.

### How it differs from nearby modes

| Mode | What it does | How a case study differs |
|---|---|---|
| Lecture / Lesson | Delivers knowledge in general form (the rule, the concept) | A case study starts from particulars and asks students to draw out or apply the general idea |
| Lab | Tests something under controlled conditions; can be re-run | A case is messy and fixed. It can only be interpreted |
| Project | Produces an artefact | A case study produces a *position* students can defend |

### The four outcomes

1. **Transfer.** Students recognise the underlying pattern in a situation they haven't seen. This is the main one. If a case study can't plausibly lead to transfer, a worked example does the job more cheaply.
2. **Applying a framework to evidence.** Students use a concept, lens or set of criteria on something real, and learn where it fits and where it strains.
3. **Decision-making under ambiguity.** The information is incomplete and the competing considerations are all legitimate.
4. **Causal reasoning.** Students ask why it happened and what would have changed it.

### Where it doesn't earn its place

- Introducing a concept cold. Students need enough of the framework first, or they have nothing to analyse with. (The exception is the contrasting-cases approach in section 2, where the cases are built to surface the distinctions.)
- Getting one right answer. If there is one, it's a worked problem.

### The design question

What should students be able to do **on a case they haven't seen**? That drives the choice of case, the questions, the sequence and the assessment.

## 2. Research overview

No single framework achieves all four outcomes. The research points to a stack, with each piece covering a different outcome.

| # | Approach | Serves | Core finding | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Contrasting cases** (Schwartz & Bransford) | Transfer, framework discovery | Analysing cases that differ in key ways *before* instruction builds differentiated knowledge, so the later explanation lands on prepared ground. A week later these students predicted outcomes better than groups who read or heard the same distinctions. The 2011 follow-up found that telling the procedure first can undermine learning of the deep structure | College students; psychology and physics. The IES project itself notes that choosing contrasting cases is "often based on intuition rather than science" |
| 2 | **Analogical encoding** (Gentner, Loewenstein, Thompson) | Transfer | Comparing two cases side by side beat studying them separately, and stronger comparison support raised transfer rates | Adult negotiation studies |
| 3 | **Multiple cases plus cueing** | Transfer | Cues helped transfer of a *structural principle* (not of a specific treatment method). Authors argue for multiple cases over a single one | Pre-professional health students |
| 4 | **Anchor case, then intermediate analogues** | Transfer | Start with a case close to what students already know, then step toward harder cases that share more features with the target | Building a good anchor case is hard |
| 5 | **Learning by Design routines** (Kolodner and colleagues) | Transfer | Defines transfer as spontaneous reminding of earlier cases. Practical routines: gallery walks, pin-up sessions, a running rules-of-thumb table | Mostly science and design contexts |
| 6 | **Standard case-method arc** (Harvard Business School, Harvard Kennedy School) | Decisions, framework application | Prep, then discussion, then debrief. Discussion is built around one learning objective in scaffolded blocks. A closing wrap-up pulls out generalisable lessons, and learning links across sessions | Business and policy settings |
| 7 | **Klein's decision tools** (recognition-primed decisions, pre-mortem, ShadowBox) | Ambiguity | Experts match a situation to past cases then mentally simulate the action. Pre-mortem: assume the decision failed and explain why. ShadowBox: commit to a decision before seeing what an expert did | No packaged classroom case structure found. Recognition-based learners need explicit scaffolding |
| 8 | **Process tracing and counterfactuals** | Causal reasoning | Process tracing examines the links between a cause and an outcome using evidence that discriminates between hypotheses. Counterfactuals fill in when no comparison case exists | Research-methods tools from political science; need translating for school. Good counterfactuals are harder to build than they look |

### From the author's own knowledge (not searched)

- **Toulmin** (claim, evidence, warrant, rebuttal) or claim-evidence-reasoning, for defending a position.
- **IRAC** (issue, rule, application, conclusion) from law, close to "apply a framework to evidence".

### Which pieces serve which outcome

| Outcome | Strongest pieces |
|---|---|
| Transfer | Contrasting or analogical cases, multiple cases, rules-of-thumb table, debrief |
| Framework applied to evidence | Contrasting cases then a named framework; IRAC or Toulmin |
| Decision under ambiguity | Commit before reveal, pre-mortem |
| Causal reasoning | Process tracing, counterfactual prompts |

## 3. The spine

Five stages, always in this order. Stages 1, 2 and 5 are always present. Stages 3 and 4 are where modules get swapped, added or dropped.

| Stage | Job |
|---|---|
| **1. Frame** | Set the question and get students' starting thinking on the table |
| **2. Meet** | Students take in the case(s) |
| **3. Work** | The main thinking |
| **4. Test** | The case pushes back |
| **5. Extract** | Students name the pattern and use it on something new |

---

## 4. The modules

### Stage 1: Frame

**F1. Pose the decision**
- *What it is:* "You are X. What would you do, and why?" Students take a first position before the case unfolds.
- *Requires:* a decision-maker role and a real choice with at least two defensible options.
- *Serves:* decision under ambiguity.

**F2. Predict**
- *What it is:* students forecast what happened or what will happen, using what they already know.
- *Requires:* a case where prior knowledge gives students something to predict from. Predictions are recorded so they can be checked at the reveal.
- *Serves:* transfer (activates prior patterns); sets up T1.

**F3. Name the lens up front**
- *What it is:* give the framework first, then use it on the case.
- *Requires:* a framework students can apply after a short introduction.
- *Serves:* framework application.
- *Use when:* the framework itself is the point of the lesson. Skip it in compared-set flows, where the aim is for students to find the distinctions first.

### Stage 2: Meet

**M1. Layered release**
- *What it is:* the case arrives in two or three instalments, with a response after each.
- *Requires:* a case that can be split at natural decision points, with each instalment adding or changing information.
- *Serves:* decision under ambiguity.

**M2. Whole case**
- *What it is:* one read, then work.
- *Requires:* a complete record with enough evidence to trace links.
- *Serves:* causal reasoning.

**M3. Case pair**
- *What it is:* two cases side by side, chosen to differ in a few deliberate ways.
- *Requires:* two cases with a shared underlying structure and a small number of clear differences. The selection of differences is the design work.
- *Serves:* transfer, framework discovery.

**M4. Anchor, then step**
- *What it is:* a familiar case first, then a case that shares more features with the target.
- *Requires:* an anchor that students already understand well, and an intermediate case that bridges to the target.
- *Serves:* transfer.

### Stage 3: Work

**W1. Notice grid**
- *What it is:* students list similarities and differences across cases *before* the concept is named.
- *Requires:* M3 (or at least two cases) and a grid with space for observations.
- *Serves:* transfer, framework discovery.

**W2. Apply a lens**
- *What it is:* students use a named framework (claim, evidence, warrant; or issue, rule, application, conclusion) on the case evidence.
- *Requires:* a framework with a student-facing scaffold (sentence frames or a template).
- *Serves:* framework application.

**W3. Trace the cause**
- *What it is:* a timeline of links, each marked strong, weak or assumed, with a column for "what evidence would change my mind".
- *Requires:* M2 (full record) and a timeline template.
- *Serves:* causal reasoning.

**W4. Counterfactual**
- *What it is:* change one thing and say what follows.
- *Requires:* a scaffold (for example: "If X had been different, then Y, because..."), since good counterfactuals are harder than they look. Works best after W3, so the real causal links are visible.
- *Serves:* causal reasoning.

**W5. Commit**
- *What it is:* a position plus a confidence rating, locked in before the reveal.
- *Requires:* a recorded response that students can't revise afterwards.
- *Serves:* decision under ambiguity; gives T1 something to push against.

### Stage 4: Test

**T1. Reveal**
- *What it is:* what actually happened, or what the expert chose, set against the student's commitment.
- *Requires:* a recorded W5 (and F2 if used), and a reveal that is genuine and sourced.
- *Serves:* decision under ambiguity, causal reasoning.

**T2. Pre-mortem**
- *What it is:* "It's two years later and this went badly. Why?"
- *Requires:* a decision on the table. Works well after W5.
- *Serves:* decision under ambiguity.

**T3. Rebuttal round**
- *What it is:* each group answers the strongest objection to its position.
- *Requires:* groups with positions (W5), and objections written or supplied.
- *Serves:* framework application (warrant and rebuttal), decision under ambiguity.

### Stage 5: Extract

**E1. Name the pattern**
- *What it is:* students write a rule of thumb in their own words, with its conditions: "when X, then Y tends to follow."
- *Requires:* a sentence frame and an expectation that the rule names its conditions.
- *Serves:* transfer.

**E2. Rules table**
- *What it is:* a class table that carries across cases. New cases confirm, narrow or break earlier rules.
- *Requires:* a persistent artefact that lives across lessons, and two or more cases.
- *Serves:* transfer.

**E3. Transfer check**
- *What it is:* a short unseen mini-case, answered independently, 10 to 15 minutes.
- *Requires:* a new case that shares structure with the studied case(s) but differs on the surface.
- *Serves:* transfer. This is the evidence that transfer happened.

### Required in every flow

- **W5 (commit).** Without a commitment, the reveal has nothing to push against.
- **E3 (transfer check).** Without a new case, transfer is an assumption.

## 5. The flows

### Flow A: Deep case

One case, one or two lessons.

**F1 → M1 or M2 → W5 → W3 + W4 → T1 → T2 or T3 → E1 → E3**

- Strongest on **causal reasoning, decision under ambiguity and framework application**.
- Weakest on **transfer**, which is why E3 is mandatory.
- Choose M1 when the focus is decision-making; choose M2 when the focus is causal tracing.

### Flow B: Compared set

Two or three cases, one or two lessons.

**F2 → M3 → W1 → *teacher names the lens* → W2 on a third case → T1 → E1 → E2 → E3**

- Strongest on **transfer and discovering the framework**.
- Weaker on depth of causal tracing, because no single case gets that much time.
- The "teacher names the lens" step comes after students have noticed the distinctions on their own.

### Flow C: Deep + Echo

Run Flow A, then add M4 and E3 as a short echo case in the same lesson or the next. The echo is where students see the pattern repeat.

### Quick comparison

| | Transfer | Framework | Ambiguity | Causal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Flow A** | weak (rescued by E3) | strong | strong | strong |
| **Flow B** | strong | strong (discovered) | moderate | weak |
| **Flow C** | moderate to strong | strong | strong | strong |

---

## 6. Across a unit

The case ladder works at unit scale:

1. **Early:** Flow A with an anchor case (M4 pairing).
2. **Mid-unit:** Flow B with contrasting cases.
3. **Assessment:** an unseen Flow A-style case, answered independently.

E2 (the rules table) runs through all of it. It is the thread that makes the second case teach more than the first.

---

## 7. Open design questions

1. **Is the Test stage always present?** It is in every flow above. It could be made optional for shorter lessons.
2. **Single deep case or small set?** Research favours sets for transfer; single cases suit causal reasoning better. The flows let the teacher choose per lesson.
3. **Which modules already exist as blocks?** Several look like things already built (for example, a hinge question for W5, a continuum line for positioning, a brainstorm for W1). Not checked against the code. Map each module to an existing block or mark it as a gap.
4. **Which scaffolds need writing first?** W2 (lens) and W3 (trace the cause) are the most likely to need a student-facing template.
5. **Case selection.** The research says choosing contrasting cases is currently intuition-led. A selection checklist (shared structure, deliberate differences, surface-feature variation) would make M3 and E3 more reliable.
