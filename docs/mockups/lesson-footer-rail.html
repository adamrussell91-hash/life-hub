Lesson footer rail and end-of-lesson questions

A rail at the bottom of every lesson: previous lesson, a tick to finish the lesson (which hands in the quiz and written work), a small feedback icon, and next lesson. Below: what the research says students can and can't judge, the questions that survive it, and a clickable mockup.

1 · The research

Students are poor judges of whether a lesson worked

Your instinct is right, and the evidence is strong. Students' sense of how much they learned, or how good a lesson was, tracks how smooth it felt, not how much they learned. The same evidence points to what students can report reliably: concrete things that happened in the room, and their own effort and confusion.

  1. 1

    Feeling of learning runs the wrong way. In randomised physics classes, students taught actively scored higher on the test but felt they had learned less than students given a polished lecture. The authors put it down to effort: students read hard thinking as a sign of not learning.

    Deslauriers et al. (2019), PNAS 116(39).

  2. 2

    Ratings reward short-term ease. With students randomly assigned to teachers, ratings predicted grades in that course but not performance in the next one. Teachers whose students did better later were rated lower.

    Carrell & West (2010), Journal of Political Economy 118(3).

  3. 3

    Overall ratings barely relate to learning. A re-analysis of 97 multi-section studies found the correlation between student ratings and learning close to zero (about r = .12 at best). It's contested, but no one in the debate argues the link is strong.

    Uttl, White & Gonzalez (2017), Studies in Educational Evaluation 54; see The Learning Scientists.

  4. 4

    Asking about what happened does work. School student surveys that ask about observable classroom events (whether the teacher checks understanding, whether time is wasted, whether you're pushed to think) predicted achievement gains and were more consistent year to year than observations. The Challenge and Control (classroom order) scales carried the most weight. The Great Teaching Toolkit surveys use the same approach: specific, observable statements.

    MET Project, Asking Students about Teaching (2012); Tripod 7Cs; Evidence Based Education on the Great Teaching Toolkit (Coe et al., 2020). A critical review warns against over-trusting these surveys for high-stakes decisions.

  5. 5

    Effort is one thing students rate well. A single "how much mental effort did that take?" item is the standard measure of cognitive load: reliable, and sensitive to small changes in task difficulty. One caution: changing the wording or the number of points weakens it.

    Paas (1992), Journal of Educational Psychology 84; reliability and validity in Instructional Science (2024) and Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas review.

  6. 6

    "How well do you understand?" right after the lesson is the least accurate time to ask. Judgements of learning made straight away are distorted by what's still in short-term memory; made after a delay, they're far better. The quiz is a better measure of understanding than any self-rating.

    Nelson & Dunlosky (1991), Psychological Science; Koriat & Bjork (2005) on illusions of competence.

  7. 7

    "What's still unclear?" is useful for planning, not as evidence of learning. The Muddiest Point is a standard classroom check: fast, anonymous-friendly and good for planning the next lesson. It records what students feel unsure of, not what they got wrong, and there's little controlled evidence that it raises achievement.

    Angelo & Cross (1993), Classroom Assessment Techniques; Cornell summary.

What that means for the questions

  1. Ask about things that happened, or about the student's own experience. Never ask them to grade the lesson, the teacher, or how much they learned.
  2. Don't read high effort as bad. Read it next to the quiz: high effort with good scores is productive struggle; high effort with low scores is overload.
  3. Leave understanding to the quiz. A self-rating straight after the lesson is the least accurate measure available.
  4. Keep it under 20 seconds and optional. One tap per question, one optional text box.
2 · Question options

The questions that hold up

Recommended: the first three, which together take about 15 seconds. The two optional ones are defensible but weaker; add them if you want the extra signal.

Recommended

use these

A · Clarity of the task

Did you know what you were meant to be doing?

  • All the way through
  • Mostly
  • Some of the time
  • Hardly at all
Why it holds
An observable event, not a quality judgement. Close to Tripod's Clarify items, which ask about specific, observable teacher actions.
What you'd do
Many "some of the time" answers point at the instructions or the order of tasks, not the content. Fix the lesson's structure.

B · Getting unstuck

When you got stuck, what happened?

  • I didn't get stuck
  • I worked it out myself
  • I got help and kept going
  • I stayed stuck
Why it holds
Reports what happened, which students can do accurately. Separates productive struggle from lost time without asking them to judge difficulty.
What you'd do
"I stayed stuck" is the one to act on: compare it with that student's quiz answers and their Markbook pattern. "Didn't get stuck" across the room together with high quiz scores may mean the lesson was too easy.

C · Still unclear (optional text)

What's one thing that's still unclear?

  • Short free text, can be left blank
Why it holds
The Muddiest Point. Diagnostic and useful for planning the next lesson. It records what students feel unsure about, so read it next to the quiz, not instead of it.
What you'd do
Feeds the start of the next lesson: a retrieval question or re-explanation aimed at the most common answer.

Optional

defensible, weaker

D · Mental effort

How hard did you have to think in this lesson?

  • Very little
  • A little
  • A fair amount
  • A lot
  • As hard as I can
Why it holds
Adapted from the Paas mental-effort scale, the best-validated self-report in this area.
Caution
The original has 9 points; this 5-point, student-friendly version hasn't been re-validated. Only useful read against quiz scores (see the rules above), never on its own, and never as "this lesson was too hard".

E · Pushed to think

In this lesson I had to explain my thinking, not just give answers.

  • Yes, a lot
  • A bit
  • Not really
Why it holds
Close to Tripod's Challenge scale, which carried the most weight in the MET results. Still an observable event.
Caution
Challenge was validated across a whole course, not one lesson. Treat one lesson's result as a hint, and watch the trend over a unit.

Left out, and why

don't ask
QuestionWhy not
Did you enjoy the lesson?Enjoyment isn't needed for learning, and a smooth, easy lesson feels better while teaching less (finding 1).
How much did you learn?Runs the wrong way: active lessons score lower on this while producing more learning (findings 1–2).
How well do you understand this now?An immediate judgement of learning, the least accurate kind (finding 6). The quiz already measures it.
Rate this lesson out of 5A global rating; close to zero relationship with learning (finding 3).
Was the lesson too hard / too easy?Asks students to judge difficulty against an ideal they can't see. Questions B and D get the same signal from things they can report.
What would make this lesson better?Asks for a teaching judgement. Answers tend toward "less writing" or "more videos".

4 · Before building

Decisions for you

  1. Which questions? Recommended A, B and C, with D and E optional.
  2. Where feedback goes. In the codebase I found the per-block pulse dots ("How's this going?") and the Feedback section, which holds feedback documents you send to students. Neither is a lesson feedback collation. Which one did you mean, or does the collation live outside the Hub (for example a Google Form)?
  3. Named or anonymous? Named lets "I stayed stuck" link to that student's Markbook row; anonymous may get more honest answers to C. A middle option is named for you only, never shown to the class.
  4. The tick can't be fully undone. A quiz in a lesson gives one go by default, so handing it in ends that go. The mockup asks once and lists anything left blank. Should students be able to untick the lesson itself (the "done" mark), even though the hand-in stays?
  5. Feedback is separate from the tick. In the mockup, the icon works any time and the tick never forces feedback. Alternatively, the feedback could open straight after the tick, still skippable.
