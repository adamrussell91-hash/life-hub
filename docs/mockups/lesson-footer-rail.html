Lesson footer rail and end-of-lesson questions

A rail at the bottom of every lesson: previous lesson, a tick to finish, next lesson. Ticking hands in the quiz and written work, then asks two quick questions about the lesson, drawn from a bank of seven, and says “Lesson completed”. Answers are anonymous and pool across students, so each lesson builds up a picture without anyone answering everything.

What changed from version 1
  • About the lesson, not the student. The effort question is gone: a student's effort says more about their day than about the lesson's design. Self-ratings of understanding stay out too.
  • A bank of ten, two asked each time. Answers pool across the class, so each question still gets answers on every lesson.
  • Anonymous. The tick is named (it's the hand-in); the feedback is stored with no link to the student.
  • One flow: tick → check what's unanswered → hand in → two questions → Lesson completed. The separate feedback icon is gone.
Version 3 · Pushback

Where version 2 didn't hold up

Version 2 took your design at face value. Tested against the research, six things need changing or watching.

  1. 1

    Ten questions won't give ten separate signals. When the Tripod survey's seven areas were tested on 25,000 students, the answers didn't split into seven. They collapsed into one general “good teaching” factor plus a separate classroom-management factor. Students who like a lesson tend to rate everything well (a halo effect). So a bank works best when each question asks about a concrete event the student could check (“did the lesson check before moving on?”), not one more angle on the same overall impression.

    Wallace, Kelcey & Ruzek (2016), AERJ 53(6); replicated in Australia by Witter & Rowe (2024).

  2. 2

    Some things change from lesson to lesson; others hardly do. Measured across one teacher's lessons, classroom management and learning support barely changed between lessons. Cognitive activation, how hard students are made to think, varied a lot, and one study estimated about nine lessons are needed to judge it reliably. So your instinct to read “explain your thinking” as a trend across a unit is right. A time-wasted question will mostly describe the class, not the lesson's design.

    Praetorius et al. (2014), Learning and Instruction 31; these were trained observers' ratings, not student ratings. The nine-lesson figure comes from a summary I couldn't check against the paper itself.

  3. 3

    Five answers is too few to trust. How reliable a class average is depends on how much students in the same class agree. With agreement at levels common in this research (an assumption I haven't checked against your classes), 5 answers gives a reliability of about .5. You need roughly 10 to 14 to reach the usual .70 bar. Ten questions with two each means about 5 answers per question per class, so most single-lesson results would sit below that. Fix: fewer questions (7 gives about 7 each), pool classes taking the same lesson, and label anything under 10 as “early read”.

    Lüdtke, Robitzsch, Trautwein & Kunter (2009), Contemporary Educational Psychology 34(2); a worked example of the same calculation in this ERIC study.

  4. 4

    The students who never tick are the ones you most need to hear from. Feedback only appears after the tick, so a lesson that ran out of time, as El Diablo Highway did, hears only from students who finished. That is non-response bias in its plainest form. Two fixes: count the share who ticked as a result in itself (it's an objective pacing signal, better than any self-report), and offer the two questions the next time a student opens a lesson they didn't finish.

  5. 5

    Every lesson is a lot of asking, and novelty fades. Repeated surveys of the same students lower response rates over time. Random picking helps for a few weeks, not a year. Watch the skip rate and how quickly answers come in, and be ready to ask on only some lessons, for example a different half of the class each time.

    Porter, Whitcomb & Weitzer (2004), Multiple surveys of students and survey fatigue.

  6. 6

    Several version 2 questions broke basic wording rules. Good survey items ask one thing, use answer options that fit the question (not agree/disagree), and keep the options in a clear order. Three failed: “could you follow the explanation” is a feeling of understanding in disguise (finding 1 of the research); “used something from an earlier lesson, and did you remember it” asks two things at once, one of them about the student; “how much of the lesson was spent on the work” asks for a proportion students can't estimate, about classmates' behaviour. “When you got stuck” has answers that aren't one ordered scale, so it needs a different chart.

    Gehlbach & Brinkworth (2011), Review of General Psychology 15(4); Panorama/Gehlbach survey checklist.

One more catch: a low answer isn't always a problem

Not every lesson should check before moving on, give examples, or end with a summary. A reading lesson may rightly ask for little explaining. So read low results against what the lesson was meant to do, and let a question be switched off for lesson types where it doesn't apply, rather than treating every bar as a score.

1 · The research

Ask about what happened in the lesson, never whether it was good

Students judge lesson quality by how smooth it felt, which runs against how much they learned. They're much better witnesses to concrete things that happened in the room. So every question in the bank asks about an observable event in the lesson.

  1. 1

    Feeling of learning runs the wrong way. In randomised physics classes, students taught actively scored higher but felt they learned less than students given a polished lecture.

    Deslauriers et al. (2019), PNAS 116(39).

  2. 2

    Overall ratings reward short-term ease and barely track learning. Highly rated teachers' students did worse in the next course; across 97 studies the link between ratings and learning was close to zero.

    Carrell & West (2010), JPE 118(3); Uttl, White & Gonzalez (2017), via The Learning Scientists.

  3. 3

    Questions about observable classroom events do predict learning. Student surveys built from specific statements (the teacher checks we understand, we're made to explain our thinking, time isn't wasted) predicted achievement gains, with Challenge and Control carrying most weight. The bank below draws on those statements and on the Great Teaching Toolkit's.

    MET Project, Asking Students about Teaching; Tripod 7Cs; Great Teaching Toolkit surveys. Caution: NEPC review.

  4. 4

    Asking each student only a few questions is an established survey design. In “matrix sampling”, each person answers a subset of the items and the results are pooled. It cuts the burden on each respondent while still covering every question. The cost is fewer answers per question, so results are read across many responses, not one lesson in one class.

    Graham, Taylor, Olchowski & Cumsille (2006), Planned missing data designs, Psychological Methods 11(4); Rhemtulla, Savalei & Little (2016).

  5. 5

    “What's still unclear?” is good for planning the next lesson. The Muddiest Point records what students feel unsure of, not what they got wrong; read it next to the quiz.

    Angelo & Cross (1993); Cornell summary.

2 · The bank

Seven questions about the lesson

Each student gets two, picked from the questions this lesson has the fewest answers to, ties broken at random, in random order. Every question asks about something the student could have seen happen.

1 · Clear task your A · kept

Did you know what you were meant to be doing?

  • All the way through
  • Mostly
  • Some of the time
  • Hardly at all

Tripod Clarify. One thing asked, ordered answers, every answer labelled.

2 · Getting unstuck your B · kept, read differently

When you got stuck, what happened?

  • I didn't get stuck
  • I worked it out myself
  • I got help and kept going
  • I stayed stuck

Report two numbers, not one bar: the share who got stuck, and of those, the share who stayed stuck. The second is the one to act on.

3 · Still unclear your C · kept

What's one thing from this lesson that's still unclear?

  • Short text
  • Nothing

Muddiest Point. Open to consider: make it a standing optional box under the two, as it's the most useful for planning the next lesson.

4 · Explain thinking your E · trend only

Did this lesson make you explain your thinking, not just give answers?

  • Yes, a lot
  • A bit
  • Not really

Tripod Challenge. Varies a lot lesson to lesson (pushback 2), so it's shown only as a unit trend.

5 · Checked before moving on

Did the lesson check whether you'd got it before moving on?

  • Yes, more than once
  • Once
  • Not that I noticed

Tripod Clarify (“checks to make sure we understand”). A clear event a student can confirm.

6 · Examples

Was there a worked example before you had to do it yourself?

  • Yes, and I used it
  • Yes, but I didn't need it
  • No

Reworded from “did the examples help” (a judgement) to whether one was there (an event). Worked examples and modelling: Great Teaching Toolkit, structuring.

7 · Earlier learning

Did this lesson bring back something from an earlier lesson?

  • Yes
  • No
  • Not sure

Split from v2: now asks only about the lesson. Whether they remembered it is the quiz's job.

3 · How the numbers add up

When a result is worth reading

≈ 7answers per question per lesson, for a class of 25 answering 2 of 7
≈ .55–.65reliability of a 7-answer average (assumed agreement, see pushback 3)
10–14answers for the usual .70 bar: two classes on the same lesson, or a few lessons pooled
% tickedshare of the class who finished: objective, every student, every lesson

Show each result with its count. Under 10 answers, label it “early read”; under 5, don't show it. The pooled view across a unit and across classes is where the numbers firm up.

6 · Before building

Still to decide

  1. The seven. Keep, reword or swap any? Should “still unclear” become a standing optional box?
  2. Students who don't finish. Offer the two questions when a student reopens an unfinished lesson? And show the share who ticked next to the results?
  3. Asking less often. Every lesson to start, then switch to half the class per lesson if the skip rate climbs?
  4. Questions that don't fit a lesson. Let you switch a question off per lesson or lesson type (for example, worked examples in a discussion lesson)?
  5. Where results appear. Beside the per-block pulse counts on the lesson, or in their own view?
  6. A pilot. Run it for a term, then drop any question whose answers barely change between lessons or barely line up with quiz results.
