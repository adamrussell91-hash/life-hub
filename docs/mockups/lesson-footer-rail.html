Lesson footer rail and end-of-lesson questions

A rail at the bottom of every lesson: previous lesson, a tick to finish, next lesson. Ticking hands in the quiz and written work, then asks two quick questions about the lesson, drawn from a bank of ten, and says “Lesson completed”. Answers are anonymous and pool across students, so each lesson builds up a picture without anyone answering everything.

What changed from version 1
  • About the lesson, not the student. The effort question is gone: a student's effort says more about their day than about the lesson's design. Self-ratings of understanding stay out too.
  • A bank of ten, two asked each time. Answers pool across the class, so each question still gets answers on every lesson.
  • Anonymous. The tick is named (it's the hand-in); the feedback is stored with no link to the student.
  • One flow: tick → check what's unanswered → hand in → two questions → Lesson completed. The separate feedback icon is gone.
1 · The research

Ask about what happened in the lesson, never whether it was good

Students judge lesson quality by how smooth it felt, which runs against how much they learned. They're much better witnesses to concrete things that happened in the room. So every question in the bank asks about an observable event in the lesson.

  1. 1

    Feeling of learning runs the wrong way. In randomised physics classes, students taught actively scored higher but felt they learned less than students given a polished lecture.

    Deslauriers et al. (2019), PNAS 116(39).

  2. 2

    Overall ratings reward short-term ease and barely track learning. Highly rated teachers' students did worse in the next course; across 97 studies the link between ratings and learning was close to zero.

    Carrell & West (2010), JPE 118(3); Uttl, White & Gonzalez (2017), via The Learning Scientists.

  3. 3

    Questions about observable classroom events do predict learning. Student surveys built from specific statements (the teacher checks we understand, we're made to explain our thinking, time isn't wasted) predicted achievement gains, with Challenge and Control carrying most weight. The bank below draws on those statements and on the Great Teaching Toolkit's.

    MET Project, Asking Students about Teaching; Tripod 7Cs; Great Teaching Toolkit surveys. Caution: NEPC review.

  4. 4

    Asking each student only a few questions is an established survey design. In “matrix sampling”, each person answers a subset of the items and the results are pooled. It cuts the burden on each respondent while still covering every question. The cost is fewer answers per question, so results are read across many responses, not one lesson in one class.

    Graham, Taylor, Olchowski & Cumsille (2006), Planned missing data designs, Psychological Methods 11(4); Rhemtulla, Savalei & Little (2016).

  5. 5

    “What's still unclear?” is good for planning the next lesson. The Muddiest Point records what students feel unsure of, not what they got wrong; read it next to the quiz.

    Angelo & Cross (1993); Cornell summary.

2 · The bank

Ten questions about the lesson

Each student gets two at the end. The two are picked from the questions this lesson has the fewest answers to so far, with ties broken at random. To a student that still feels random, and every question gets answers on every lesson.

1 · Clear task your A

Did you know what you were meant to be doing?

  • All the way through
  • Mostly
  • Some of the time
  • Hardly at all

Tripod Clarify. Low answers point at the instructions or the order of tasks.

2 · Getting unstuck your B

When you got stuck, what happened?

  • I didn't get stuck
  • I worked it out myself
  • I got help and kept going
  • I stayed stuck

Tripod Clarify (“explains it another way”). Many “stayed stuck” answers point at the lesson's support or examples.

3 · Still unclear your C

What's one thing from this lesson that's still unclear?

  • Short text
  • Nothing

Muddiest Point. Feeds the start of the next lesson.

4 · Explain thinking your E

In this lesson, did you have to explain your thinking, not just give answers?

  • Yes, a lot
  • A bit
  • Not really

Tripod Challenge, the strongest predictor. Read as a trend across the unit.

5 · Following explanations

When something new was explained, could you follow it?

  • Easily
  • Mostly
  • Only after help
  • Not really

Tripod Clarify (“explains difficult things clearly”).

6 · Examples

Did the examples help you do the task yourself?

  • Yes
  • Partly
  • Not really
  • There weren't any

Worked examples and modelling, from the Great Teaching Toolkit's structuring dimension.

7 · Checked before moving on

Did the lesson check whether you'd got it before moving on?

  • Yes, more than once
  • Once
  • Not that I noticed

Tripod Clarify (“checks to make sure we understand”).

8 · Earlier learning

Did this lesson use something from an earlier lesson?

  • Yes, and I remembered it
  • Yes, but I'd forgotten it
  • No
  • Not sure

Great Teaching Toolkit (“helps us remember ideas from previous lessons”). “Forgotten” answers flag what to bring back.

9 · Time on the work

How much of the lesson was spent on the actual work?

  • Nearly all
  • Most
  • About half
  • Less than half

Tripod Control (“we don't waste time”), the other strong predictor.

10 · Pulled together

Did the lesson end by pulling the main ideas together?

  • Yes
  • Sort of
  • No

Tripod Consolidate (“summarises what we learned”).

Green edge: the four you picked. Amber edge: new ones, built the same way (each asks about something that happened in the lesson). Drop any you don't want; the bank works with eight or more.

3 · How the numbers add up

Enough answers to read, without asking everyone everything

25 × 2students × questions each = 50 answers per lesson per class
≈ 5answers per question per lesson, with ten questions
≈ 45answers per question across a nine-lesson unit
5the fewest answers before a result is shown; below that it says “not enough yet”

One lesson in one class gives a rough read; it gets solid when the same lesson runs with more than one class or as answers build up over a unit. That suits question 4 especially, which you'd read as a trend.

6 · Before building

Still to decide

  1. The bank. Keep all ten, or drop some? Any wording changes?
  2. Where results appear. I couldn't find a lesson feedback collation in the Hub. The per-block “How's this going?” dots are counted in the lesson editor, and the Feedback section holds documents you send to students. Should results sit beside the pulse counts on the lesson, in their own view, or somewhere outside the Hub?
  3. How anonymous. Feedback is stored with no student id. To stop one device sending feedback twice for a lesson, the server keeps a one-way hash of the device, which can't be traced back to a name. In a small class, free-text answers can still give someone away by how they write.
  4. Skipping. In the mockup, students can press “Skip” and still finish. Should skipping be allowed?
  5. Unticking. The quiz hand-in can't be undone (one go by default). Can a student untick the lesson's “done” mark itself?
